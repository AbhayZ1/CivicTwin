\begin{abstract}
Ex-ante policy comparison asks which of several candidate interventions a city
should prefer, before any of them is enacted. We treat this as a problem of
causal inference under spatial interference rather than as one of forecasting.
An intervention applied to one neighbourhood alters rents in the neighbourhoods
adjacent to it, so the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption fails, and an
estimator which ignores the failure attributes the neighbour response to the
control condition.

We propose the Causal Graph Counterfactual Exposure Network (\CGCEN{}), in which
the one-step-ahead prediction for a neighbourhood splits into a direct term
$\Phi$, evaluated at the unit itself, and an interference term obtained by
aggregating a second function $\Psi$ over the neighbours with row-normalised
weights. The two channels are kept apart by the architecture and not by a
penalty: $\Phi$ reads a temporal encoding which no message-passing operator has
touched, so neighbour state cannot reach it. Contrasting each channel against
its untreated counterfactual returns an individual treatment effect which is
zero at every untreated unit and an interference effect which is zero on an
edgeless graph. Alongside the model we define a housing-plus-transportation
overburden index and a displacement pressure index carrying a cumulative
burden term.

On a synthetic city with a known generating process, over ten seeds and four
policy scenarios, \CGCEN{} attains pooled MAE $8.88 \pm 2.40$, against
$11.87 \pm 3.12$ for a spatial lag model, $20.03 \pm 7.97$ for a temporal MLP
which cannot see the graph, and $27.90 \pm 1.27$ for persistence. The margin
over the spatial lag model is significant under each of the three interventions
($p = 0.0015$ to $0.0033$) and is not significant in the untreated equilibrium
($p = 0.127$), which we report rather than pool away. Deleting the edges costs
$2.89$ MAE and permuting them costs $4.88$, so a wrong graph does more damage
than no graph. We also report a negative result which shaped the design. Under
a generator whose spillover arrives once and stays, no model we tried gained
anything from the graph, because spillover of that kind is absorbed into the
level of each unit and becomes a fixed effect. Graph learning repays its cost
here only when the spillover process keeps moving.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{U}{rban} policy is written for parcels and districts, and its
consequences ignore those boundaries. Upzoning a corridor, relaxing parking
minima or capturing land value to pay for transit changes rents and land values
inside the treated zone, and it also changes them next door, through housing
search, through commuting, and through what developers expect to happen next.
For the question a planner actually asks, which is who is displaced and where,
the diffusion is not a nuisance. It is the object of interest.

\textbf{Interference and SUTVA.} The potential outcomes framework rests on the
Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption \cite{rubin1980}, under which the outcome
of a unit depends on the treatment given to that unit and on nothing else.
Spatial spillover breaks the assumption outright. A tract next to an upzoned
corridor has been partially treated although it received no intervention, and an
estimator which files it under the control condition will read the rent movement
there as noise, or as evidence that the policy did less than it did. The
literature on partial interference \cite{hudgens2008,ogburn2014,forastiere2021}
handles this by letting the outcome depend on a low dimensional summary of the
neighbours' assignments. We take that route and put the summary in a learned
spatio-temporal model.

\textbf{Comparison, not prediction.} There is a second difficulty, less often
discussed. Work on urban and mobility data usually poses the task as
forecasting: what will rents be next month, what will traffic be next hour.
Spatio-temporal graph networks are good at this \cite{li2018dcrnn,yu2018stgcn}.
But an agency choosing between an inclusionary mandate and a land-value capture
scheme is not asking what rents will be in 2031. It is asking which of several
worlds, none of which has happened, is preferable, and for whom. A point
forecast can be accurate and useless for that purpose, because it cannot be
conditioned on an intervention which has not occurred.

We therefore fix the scope of the paper as ex-ante comparison. The quantity of
interest is not $\hat{Y}_{i,t+1}$ on its own but the difference between
$\hat{Y}_{i,t+1}$ under an intervention and under the untreated counterfactual,
taken together with the same difference at the neighbours of $i$. Forecast
accuracy enters as a necessary condition and not as the goal. A model whose
conditional expectations are poorly calibrated cannot be trusted to produce
differences between them.

\textbf{\CGCEN{}: an introduction.} Let $w_i \in \{0,1\}$ record whether
neighbourhood $i$ receives the intervention. Write $\mathcal{N}(i)$ for the
neighbours of $i$ and $W_{ij}$ for the row-normalised adjacency weights. The
model predicts
\begin{equation}
  \hat{Y}_{i,t+1} \;=\; \Phi(X_{i,t}, P_{i,t})
  \;+\; \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} W_{ij} \cdot \Psi(X_{j,t}, P_{j,t}),
  \label{eq:intro-split}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ carries the individual treatment effect and $\Psi$, once
aggregated, carries the spatial interference effect. Nothing in
\eqref{eq:intro-split} is unusual as an equation. What matters is where the two
functions get their arguments from. The encoding fed to $\Phi$ is produced by a
recurrent pass over the history of unit $i$ alone, with no message passing
anywhere in it, so there is no route by which the state of a neighbour can enter
the direct term. The encoding fed to $\Psi$ is evaluated at the neighbours and
aggregated across edges, and it is the only such route. The separation is a
property of the wiring, not of a penalty which might or might not bind.

Two identities follow at once, and we use both as regression tests. If
$P_{i,t}=0$ then the treatment effect at $i$ is exactly zero, because the treated
and untreated arguments of $\Phi$ coincide. If the edge set is empty then the
interference effect is exactly zero, because the sum in \eqref{eq:intro-split}
is empty. Neither identity is learned and neither can drift.

\textbf{Why a synthetic city.} Credible spatial policy data with a known ground
truth is scarce, and evaluating a spillover-aware estimator on observational
data is close to circular: the spillover structure is exactly what is unknown,
so a model which appears to exploit it cannot be told apart from one which has
fitted confounding. We therefore build a generator whose process we specify
completely, which lets us ask a question the observational setting cannot
answer. When the spillover is known to be there, does the graph model find it?
The answer turned out to depend on what kind of spillover it is, and that
dependence is the main thing we have to report.

\textbf{Summary of results.} The contributions are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
\item[--] Section \ref{sec:model} gives the architecture in which the direct and
interference channels are separated by construction, together with the
counterfactual contrasts which recover the two effects and the two degenerate
cases in which they vanish exactly.

\item[--] Section \ref{sec:model} also revises the displacement pressure index.
An index built only from growth rates cannot register a sustained policy effect:
a permanent shift in level moves the growth rate once and then the index returns
to its old path. Adding a cumulative burden term raises the measured contrast
between scenarios by more than two orders of magnitude, from around $10^{-4}$ to
around $1.7 \times 10^{-2}$.

\item[--] Section \ref{sec:generator} reports the negative result. Under a
generator in which the spillover is a single permanent shock, no model gained
from the graph. Deleting the edges improved the network, permuting them changed
nothing, and the learned spatial coefficient of the spatial lag model went to
zero. We give the mechanism, which is that early permanent spillover is
absorbed into the level of the receiving unit and is thereafter
indistinguishable from a fixed effect.

\item[--] Section \ref{sec:experiments} reports the benchmark on the repaired
generator. \CGCEN{} is ahead of every baseline pooled over scenarios, and its
margin over the spatial lag model is significant under intervention
($p = 0.0015$ to $0.0033$) but not in the untreated equilibrium
($p = 0.127$). Permuting the edges is worse than deleting them.

\item[--] The generator, the four-model baseline suite, the ten-seed harness
with confidence intervals and paired tests, and the topology ablations are
released, along with a configuration profile which reproduces the degenerate
regime so that the negative result stays auditable.
\end{itemize}

We put some weight on the third item. It is the kind of finding which normally
disappears during model development, and had it disappeared here we would have
published a paper claiming that a graph network captures urban spillover, on a
benchmark which contained no spillover it could capture. Reporting where the
boundary lies seems more useful than reporting only the side of it on which the
method wins.

\textbf{Related work.} Three lines of work meet here. Causal inference under
interference relaxes SUTVA by allowing the outcome of a unit to depend on the
assignments of others. Hudgens and Halloran \cite{hudgens2008} formalise partial
interference within disjoint clusters, Ogburn and VanderWeele \cite{ogburn2014}
give a graphical treatment, and Forastiere et al. \cite{forastiere2021} extend
identification to observational networks through an explicit exposure mapping.
We borrow the exposure mapping and place it inside a learned model rather than a
parametric outcome regression.

Neural counterfactual estimation learns representations for treatment effect
estimation, usually under SUTVA. Shalit et al. \cite{shalit2017cfr} give
generalisation bounds for individual effects and Wager and Athey
\cite{wager2018causalforest} give inference for forest-based heterogeneous
effects. Closest to our setting is Ma and Tresp \cite{ma2021netdeconf}, who
carry neural counterfactual estimation to networked interference. What we add
relative to that line is the architectural separation of the two channels and
the temporal dimension.

Spatio-temporal graph learning has been driven largely by traffic forecasting.
Kipf and Welling \cite{kipf2017gcn} introduced the graph convolution we take as
a reference point, and DCRNN \cite{li2018dcrnn} and STGCN \cite{yu2018stgcn} set
the recurrent and convolutional templates. These are forecasting models fitted
to observational series. Neither exposes a treatment channel or a counterfactual
contrast, which is what comparison requires. On the measurement side we follow
the combined housing and transportation framing of the H$+$T index
\cite{cnt2023ht}, and our displacement indicators are informed by Zuk et al.
\cite{zuk2018gentrification}, who document that public investment can raise
accessibility and displacement risk at the same time. That tension is what the
H$+$T index is built to expose.

\textbf{Notation.} Units are indexed by $i$ and $j$ and time steps by $t$. We
write $X_{i,t} \in \mathbb{R}^{F}$ for the feature vector of unit $i$ at step
$t$, $P_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}$ for the policy assignment, $d_i$ for the degree of
$i$, and $W_h$ for the length of the history window. Errors are always pooled
over windows, units and features before they are reduced, so RMSE below is a
root mean squared error and not a root mean square of per-window absolute
errors. Standard deviations use the sample convention and confidence intervals
are Student-$t$ at the $95\%$ level. All comparisons between models are paired
on the seed.

\section{The model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Graph and exposure}

Let $G = (V, E, W)$ be an undirected weighted graph on $|V| = N$ units, a census
tract in the empirical setting and a lattice cell in the synthetic one, with
edges recording spatial adjacency. Write $\mathcal{N}(i)$ for the neighbours of
$i$. The weight matrix is the row-normalised adjacency,
\begin{equation}
  W_{ij} =
  \begin{cases}
    a_{ij} \big/ \sum_{k} a_{ik}, & (i,j) \in E,\\[2pt]
    0, & \text{otherwise,}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}
so that $\sum_{j} W_{ij} = 1$ at every unit of positive degree. Normalising the
rows turns the interference term into an average rather than a sum, which keeps
its scale free of the degree and stops high degree units from dominating the
objective.

Under SUTVA the potential outcome of unit $i$ would be written $Y_i(P_i)$.
Spatial spillover makes that inadequate, and we adopt instead the partial
interference assumption, under which the outcome depends on the assignment of
$i$ and on the neighbours' assignments only through a summary. We use the
degree-normalised exposure
\begin{equation}
  e_i \;=\; \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} W_{ij} P_{j,t}
        \;=\; \frac{1}{d_i} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} P_{j,t},
  \label{eq:exposure}
\end{equation}
so $e_i \in [0,1]$ is the treated fraction of the neighbourhood of $i$. The
potential outcome becomes $Y_i(P_i, e_i)$, and the estimands separate into a
direct effect at fixed exposure and an interference effect at fixed own
treatment.

\subsection{The two channels}

Let $h_i \in \mathbb{R}^{H}$ be a temporal encoding of the history of unit $i$,
built by a shared encoder followed by a gated recurrent unit \cite{cho2014gru},
\begin{align}
  z_{i,k} &= \mathrm{ReLU}\!\left( \mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{enc}} \tilde{X}_{i,k}
             + b_{\mathrm{enc}} \right), \quad k = t-W_h+1, \dots, t,
             \label{eq:encoder}\\
  h_i     &= \mathrm{GRU}\!\left( z_{i,t-W_h+1}, \dots, z_{i,t} \right),
             \label{eq:gru}
\end{align}
where $\tilde{X}$ denotes the standardised features of
Section~\ref{subsec:scaling}. Note that \eqref{eq:encoder} and \eqref{eq:gru}
contain no message passing of any kind, so $h_i$ is a function of the history of
$i$ and of nothing else.

Writing $s_i = [\, h_i \,\Vert\, P_{i,t} \,]$ for the state concatenated with
the assignment, the prediction is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{Y}_{i,t+1}
  = \underbrace{X_{i,t} + \mathrm{MLP}_{\Phi}(s_i)}_{\Phi(X_{i,t}, P_{i,t})}
  \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} W_{ij} \,
    \mathrm{MLP}_{\Psi}(s_j)}_{\text{interference}} .
  \label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}
The leading $X_{i,t}$ inside $\Phi$ is a residual anchor: the network is asked
for the change from the last observation rather than for the level. This is not
cosmetic. Without it the recurrent unit has to rebuild an absolute level through
a saturating $\tanh$ bottleneck, and the near-linear part of the signal, which
is simply that the next value resembles the last one, is destroyed on the way.
Ablating the anchor and holding everything else fixed moves pooled MAE from
$8.92$ to $37.28$, a factor of $4.2$. Since $X_{i,t}$ is a function of the state
of $i$, the anchor belongs to the direct term and does not contaminate the other
one.

\subsection{Recovering the two effects}

Effects are recovered by contrasting each channel against its own untreated
counterfactual. Put $s_i^{0} = [\, h_i \,\Vert\, 0 \,]$ and set
\begin{align}
  \tau_i &= \mathrm{MLP}_{\Phi}(s_i) - \mathrm{MLP}_{\Phi}(s_i^{0}),
  \label{eq:ite}\\
  \eta_i &= \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} W_{ij}
            \left( \mathrm{MLP}_{\Psi}(s_j) - \mathrm{MLP}_{\Psi}(s_j^{0}) \right),
  \label{eq:ste}
\end{align}
where $\tau_i$ is the estimated individual treatment effect and $\eta_i$ the
estimated spatial interference effect. If $P_{i,t}=0$ then $s_i = s_i^{0}$ and
$\tau_i = 0$ exactly. If $E = \varnothing$ then the sum in \eqref{eq:ste} is
empty and $\eta_i = 0$ exactly. The additive identity
$\hat{Y}_{i,t+1} = \Phi_i + \sum_j W_{ij}\Psi_j$ holds to floating point
tolerance by construction. Algorithm~\ref{alg:cgcen} states the forward pass
together with the contrasts.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\CGCEN{} forward pass with direct and spillover decomposition}
\label{alg:cgcen}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE History $X_{i,t-W_h+1:t}$ for all $i \in V$; assignment
         $P_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}$; neighbourhoods $\mathcal{N}(\cdot)$ with
         row-normalised weights $W_{ij}$
\ENSURE  Prediction $\hat{Y}_{i,t+1}$, effects $\tau_i$ and $\eta_i$
\STATE $\mu, \sigma \gets$ \textsc{FitScaler}$(X_{\text{train}})$
       \COMMENT{training split only}
\STATE $\tilde{X}_{i,\cdot} \gets (X_{i,\cdot} - \mu) / \sigma$ for all $i$
\FOR{$k = t-W_h+1$ \TO $t$}
    \STATE $z_{i,k} \gets \mathrm{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{enc}} \tilde{X}_{i,k}
           + b_{\mathrm{enc}})$
\ENDFOR
\STATE $h_i \gets \mathrm{GRU}(z_{i,t-W_h+1}, \dots, z_{i,t})$
       \COMMENT{no message passing}
\STATE $s_i \gets [\, h_i \,\Vert\, P_{i,t} \,]$, \quad
       $s_i^{0} \gets [\, h_i \,\Vert\, 0 \,]$
\STATE $\Phi_i \gets X_{i,t} + \mathrm{MLP}_{\Phi}(s_i)$
       \COMMENT{residual anchor}
\STATE $\Omega_i \gets \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} W_{ij}\,\mathrm{MLP}_{\Psi}(s_j)$
\STATE $\hat{Y}_{i,t+1} \gets \Phi_i + \Omega_i$
\STATE $\tau_i \gets \mathrm{MLP}_{\Phi}(s_i) - \mathrm{MLP}_{\Phi}(s_i^{0})$
\STATE $\eta_i \gets \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} W_{ij}
        \big(\mathrm{MLP}_{\Psi}(s_j) - \mathrm{MLP}_{\Psi}(s_j^{0})\big)$
\RETURN $\hat{Y}_{i,t+1}, \tau_i, \eta_i$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Scaling}
\label{subsec:scaling}

The panel spans several orders of magnitude. Median income is of order $10^{4}$
and accessibility is of order $10^{0}$, so an unscaled squared error objective
is governed by the large columns and the gradient carried by the rest is lost.
We standardise each feature, $\tilde{X}_{\cdot,\cdot,f} =
(X_{\cdot,\cdot,f} - \mu_f)/\sigma_f$, with $\mu_f$ and $\sigma_f$ estimated on
the training split only and reused unchanged on the test split, so that no
distributional information crosses the temporal boundary backwards. Constant
features are given $\sigma_f = 1$. Metrics are computed after inverting the
transform, which keeps the errors in the original units and comparable across
configurations.

\subsection{Baselines}

We compare against three baselines chosen to span the relevant hypothesis space.
The \emph{temporal MLP} flattens the history window and maps it through a
two-layer perceptron. It cannot see the graph, and it isolates what the topology
is worth. The \emph{spatial lag model} is a learned form of the classical
spatial autoregressive specification \cite{anselin1988,lesage2009},
\begin{equation}
  \hat{Y}_{i,t+1} = X_{i,t} + \beta^{\top} \mathrm{vec}(X_{i,t-W_h+1:t})
  + \rho \odot \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} W_{ij} X_{j,t},
  \label{eq:slm}
\end{equation}
with a learned spatial coefficient $\rho$ and a residual anchor matching
\eqref{eq:decomposition}, so that the comparison turns on functional form and
not on parameterisation. The \emph{persistence} model returns
$\hat{Y}_{i,t+1} = X_{i,t}$, has no parameters and is never trained. It is the
floor which any learned model must clear before a claim about spillover is worth
making, and including it is what told us, at an earlier stage of this work, that
both learned baselines were below it.

\subsection{Two indices}

\textbf{Housing and transportation.} Housing cost on its own understates what
households in poorly connected neighbourhoods pay, because a low rent is often
bought with a long commute. We define
\begin{equation}
  \HT_{i,t}
  = \frac{\mathrm{Rent}_{i,t} + \mathrm{TransitCost}_{i,t}}
         {\mathrm{MedianIncome}_{i,t}} ,
  \label{eq:ht}
\end{equation}
with transit cost falling in accessibility,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{TransitCost}_{i,t} =
  \frac{c_0}{\max(\mathrm{Accessibility}_{i,t}, \epsilon)^{\kappa}} ,
  \label{eq:transit}
\end{equation}
for a base cost $c_0$ and an elasticity $\kappa$. This is the mechanism a
rent-to-income ratio misses. A transit investment which raises accessibility
lowers $\HT$ even where it raises rent, and whether the net movement is
progressive is then a question the index can be asked.

\textbf{Displacement pressure.} The index blends adverse movements in rent, in
low income share and in accessibility,
\begin{align}
  \DPI_{i,t} =\;& \alpha \left( \frac{\Delta \mathrm{Rent}_{i,t}}
                                    {\mathrm{Rent}_{i,t-1}} \right)
  + \beta \left( 1 - \frac{\mathrm{LowIncomeShare}_{i,t}}
                          {\mathrm{LowIncomeShare}_{i,t-1}} \right) \nonumber\\
  &+ \gamma \left( \Delta \mathrm{Accessibility}_{i,t} \right)
   + \delta \, B_{i,t} .
  \label{eq:dpi}
\end{align}
The first three terms are the usual growth-rate form. The fourth is a
correction, and the reason for it is worth stating plainly. A growth-rate index
cannot see a sustained level difference. A policy which raises rents permanently
by six per cent moves the growth rate in the single period in which the shift
happens, after which the index returns to the path it was already on. In our
runs this left every policy contrast at around $10^{-4}$, present in the
arithmetic and absent in any useful sense. We therefore add a cumulative burden
term measured against the initial burden of the same unit,
\begin{equation}
  B_{i,t} = \frac{\HT_{i,t}}{\max(\HT_{i,0}, \epsilon)} - 1 ,
  \label{eq:burden}
\end{equation}
which is zero at $t = 0$ and keeps a permanent offset under a permanent shift.
Setting $\delta = 0$ recovers the growth-rate form exactly, and we pin that with
a test so the original specification stays checkable.

Both indices are constructed comparative indicators over a controlled panel.
Neither is a calibrated forecast of realised displacement, and we use them only
to rank scenarios against a common baseline.

\section{The synthetic city}
\label{sec:generator}

\subsection{Graph and dynamics}

The city is a $4 \times 4$ lattice of $N = 16$ units with four-connected
adjacency, which gives $24$ undirected edges, $48$ directed entries and degrees
between two and four. Each unit carries land value, rent, population, median
income, housing units and accessibility, initialised with multiplicative
Gaussian heterogeneity around city-wide bases of $100$, $50$, $2000$, $60{,}000$,
$900$ and $1.0$ respectively. Generation is seeded and reproducible.

The dynamics are deliberately transparent. Accessibility shocks raise land
values, land value growth feeds rent growth after a lag, rent pressure damps
population, income and housing growth, and neighbour terms diffuse land and rent
across the edges. A designated set $S = \{0,1,4,7\}$ receives a permanent
accessibility uplift from $t \ge 3$, standing in for a transit investment.

\subsection{A negative result: the static intercept}
\label{subsec:collapse}

Our first generator used only that permanent shock, and under it no model
benefited from the graph. We give the evidence before the explanation.

\begin{itemize}
\item[--] The spatial lag coefficient of \eqref{eq:slm} converged to
$\rho \approx 0$ across seeds, taking values $-0.05$, $-0.07$ and $0.02$.
\item[--] \CGCEN{} with the edges deleted was \emph{better} than \CGCEN{} on the
real graph, $14.16$ against $14.82$ MAE.
\item[--] Permuting the edges was indistinguishable from the true topology,
with $p$ between $0.70$ and $0.93$.
\end{itemize}

The mechanism is instructive, and once seen it is obvious. A shock applied once
at $t = 3$ to a fixed set of units means that by the evaluation window, which
begins at $t = 12$, the neighbour contribution at each unit has settled to a
constant. That constant is folded into the level of the unit and is recovered
perfectly by the residual anchor $X_{i,t}$ of \eqref{eq:decomposition}. Put
differently, spillover which is both permanent and early is observationally the
same thing as a unit fixed effect. There is nothing left for a graph to carry,
because the information has already arrived by another road. We call this the
static intercept, and we suspect it is a common and under-reported failure of
spatio-temporal benchmarks which inject spillover as a single regime change.

\subsection{Dynamic shocks}

The repair is to keep the spillover moving. At each step every unit
independently receives an accessibility pulse with probability
$p_{\mathrm{pulse}} = 0.22$, of magnitude drawn uniformly from
$[0.5\sigma_p, 1.5\sigma_p]$ with $\sigma_p = 1.2$,
\begin{equation}
  u_{i,t} \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_{\mathrm{pulse}}) \cdot
                \mathcal{U}(0.5\sigma_p,\, 1.5\sigma_p).
  \label{eq:pulse}
\end{equation}
The pulse enters the observed accessibility of the unit at $t$, and, which is
the point of the construction, it reaches the rent growth of the
\emph{neighbours} at $t+1$,
\begin{equation}
  g^{\mathrm{rent}}_{i,t} \;\mathrel{+}=\; \lambda_p \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)}
  W_{ij} \, u_{j,t-1},
  \label{eq:pulse-transmission}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_p = 0.22$. Because the pulse is transient and random, a pulse at a
neighbour of $i$ cannot be inferred from the history of $i$. It is new
information which travels along an edge and nowhere else. Accessibility is
observed, so the pulse at $j$ is visible at time $t$ to a model which can read
across the edge, and it predicts the rent of $i$ at $t+1$. This is what makes
the graph necessary rather than merely available.

\subsection{Carrying capacities}

The first generator applied purely multiplicative growth with nothing to
equilibrate it, and this produced a second defect, worse than the first. Rents
compounded without bound. As the rent-to-income ratio grew, the rent pressure
term in the income update drove income sharply down, and income met its hard
floor. In the unrepaired generator income reached the floor at $t = 52$, and by
$t = 100$ every one of the sixteen units was pinned there while mean rent had
compounded to about $4 \times 10^{10}$. Any statistic taken past $t \approx 52$
was measuring the clamp and not the economy.

We add a logistic carrying capacity. Each growth rate is multiplied by a
headroom factor which vanishes as the state approaches its capacity,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{headroom}(x, K) = \max\!\left(0,\; 1 - \frac{x}{K}\right),
  \label{eq:headroom}
\end{equation}
with land capacity $12$ times the base land value, income capacity $4$ times the
base income, and a rent capacity tied to contemporaneous ability to pay,
$K_R = 0.012 \times \mathrm{Income}_{i,t-1}$. Rent and income also carry mean
reversion, towards $0.55 K_R$ and towards the base income, at rates $0.05$ and
$0.02$.

The economic reading is a supply response: as rents approach the local
affordability ceiling, further growth is choked off by construction, by vacancy
and by out-migration. The numerical effect is that the economy settles instead
of diverging. Under the revised generator no unit reaches any hard floor through
$t = 100$ on any of the ten seeds. Mean rent settles at about
$1.62 \times 10^{3}$, income at about $1.50 \times 10^{5}$ and $\HT$ at about
$0.011$. Figure~\ref{fig:trajectories} shows the trajectories.

\subsection{Scenarios}

We define four scenarios, applied to the boundary $S$ as an intervention on the
generated panel. All four are simple and additive, so that the direction each
one should move the indices is not in doubt.

\begin{itemize}
\item[--] \textbf{Baseline.} No intervention. Supplies the untreated
counterfactual against which the others are differenced.
\item[--] \textbf{Market-led development.} Upzoning with no affordability
condition attached: land values up $4\%$ and rents up $6\%$ per unit intensity
from $t \ge 2$. Should raise displacement pressure.
\item[--] \textbf{Inclusionary housing.} New development tied to affordable set
asides: rents down $6\%$ and stock up $8\%$ per unit intensity from $t \ge 1$.
Should lower pressure.
\item[--] \textbf{Land-value capture.} Taxation of uplift recycled into
affordability: land values up a damped $2\%$ and rents down $5\%$ per unit
intensity from $t \ge 2$. Should lower pressure, with a different land and rent
signature from the inclusionary case.
\end{itemize}

Intensities of $0.8$, $0.6$ and $0.7$ are configuration parameters and are not
fitted. For each of ten seeds we export all four panels with the adjacency, the
edge index, the low income share and the treated boundary, giving forty
datasets.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Protocol}

Each run generates a seeded city of $N = 16$ units over $T = 18$ steps, applies
one of the four scenarios, and forms rolling history windows of length
$W_h = 4$ with next-step targets. This gives fourteen windows. The split is
temporal, the earliest $70\%$ for training and the rest for testing, with no
shuffling, so nine windows train and five are held out and no future information
reaches the training set. The scaler of Section~\ref{subsec:scaling} is fitted
on the training windows alone.

We model the three channels which carry the spillover, namely land value, rent
and accessibility. Every model is constructed under an identical seed
immediately before instantiation, so that a comparison between architectures, or
between topology conditions, reflects what is being varied and not the draw of
the initial weights. Learned models train for $250$ epochs with Adam at learning
rate $10^{-3}$ under a squared error objective. Persistence has no parameters
and is not trained. Metrics are computed after inverting the scaler.

The design is ten seeds by four scenarios by four models for the main sweep,
together with ten seeds by four scenarios by two ablated topologies for
\CGCEN{}. This is $240$ model instances of which $200$ are trained, the
remaining forty being persistence.

\subsection{Accuracy}

Table~\ref{tab:benchmark} gives MAE and RMSE by scenario and
Figure~\ref{fig:comparison} summarises the pooled comparison. Pooled over the
four scenarios on the real graph, \CGCEN{} reaches $\mathrm{MAE} = 8.88 \pm
2.40$, against $11.87 \pm 3.12$ for the spatial lag model, $20.03 \pm 7.97$ for
the temporal MLP and $27.90 \pm 1.27$ for persistence. The RMSE ordering is the
same, at $13.81$, $19.30$, $34.54$ and $37.54$, so the advantage is not an
artefact of a few well-predicted units and survives a loss which weights large
residuals more heavily.

The margin over the graph-blind MLP is large and significant in every scenario,
with mean differences between $-10.25$ and $-12.45$ MAE and $p$ between
$9.4 \times 10^{-4}$ and $5.8 \times 10^{-3}$. The margin over persistence is
larger again, with $p < 2 \times 10^{-8}$ throughout. We treat the persistence
comparison as a sanity floor and not as a result.

\subsection{Where the graph pays}

The comparison against the spatial lag model is the substantive one, since that
model is itself graph-aware and differs from \CGCEN{} chiefly in functional
form. Here the result is conditional, and we give the condition rather than the
pooled average.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Scenario} & $\Delta$\textbf{MAE} & $t$ & $p$ & $d_z$ \\
\midrule
Baseline              & $-1.94$ & $-1.68$ & $0.127$  & $-0.53$ \\
Market-led            & $-3.34$ & $-4.14$ & $0.0025$ & $-1.31$ \\
Inclusionary housing  & $-3.15$ & $-3.96$ & $0.0033$ & $-1.25$ \\
Land-value capture    & $-3.53$ & $-4.50$ & $0.0015$ & $-1.42$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\CGCEN{} is ahead by roughly $3.3$ MAE under each intervention, with paired
effect sizes between $-1.25$ and $-1.42$, and its advantage of $1.94$ MAE in the
untreated equilibrium does not reach significance at ten seeds.

We think this boundary is the most useful thing in the paper. In a static
equilibrium the spatial structure of the system is summarised well enough by a
linear autoregressive lag, and the extra capacity of a nonlinear message-passing
model buys nothing which survives the variance between seeds. Only when the
interventions inject treatment-dependent perturbations which propagate does the
interference channel have anything to work on. A paper which reported only the
pooled comparison would have claimed a general forecasting advantage which the
per-scenario decomposition does not support.

\subsection{Topology ablation}

To establish that the advantage comes from the graph and not from capacity, we
refit \CGCEN{} under two counterfactual topologies with everything else held
fixed. Under \emph{no edges} the interference term of \eqref{eq:decomposition}
is identically zero. Under \emph{shuffled} the edge index is remapped through a
random permutation of the units, which preserves the degree sequence and the
edge count and destroys the correspondence between the graph and space.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrl}
\toprule
\textbf{Topology} & \textbf{MAE} & \textbf{Loss} & \textbf{$p$ vs.\ real} \\
\midrule
Real graph      & $8.88$  & ---     & --- \\
No edges        & $11.77$ & $+2.89$ & $0.005$ to $0.008$ (3 of 4) \\
Shuffled edges  & $13.76$ & $+4.88$ & $0.0001$ to $0.018$ (4 of 4) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Deleting the graph costs $2.89$ MAE, a relative loss of $32.6\%$. Permuting it
costs $4.88$, a loss of $55.0\%$, significant in all four scenarios. The
per-scenario breakdown is in Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}, and the one scenario in
which edge deletion falls short of significance is the untreated baseline, at
$p = 0.080$, which is consistent with the previous subsection.

The observation which matters is that shuffled is worse than no edges. Were the
model using message passing as a generic smoother, a random graph would serve
about as well as the true one and both would sit between the real graph and the
edgeless case. Instead a structurally plausible but factually wrong topology
does more damage than supplying none. That is the signature of a model which has
learned the particular adjacency: it propagates neighbour information with
confidence, and when the information comes from the wrong neighbours the
confidence is what hurts.

For contrast, the same ablation under the static-shock generator of
Section~\ref{subsec:collapse} put no edges at $14.16$ against $14.82$ for the
real graph, the edgeless model being ahead, with shuffled indistinguishable from
real. The ablation is therefore not a confirmation of the final model. It is the
diagnostic which separated a working benchmark from a degenerate one, and we
would encourage treating the shuffled condition as routine rather than optional.

\subsection{The two channels}

Figure~\ref{fig:spillover} shows the estimated effects on the lattice. The
treatment effect concentrates on the treated boundary, as it must, since
$\tau_i$ is identically zero at untreated units by \eqref{eq:ite}. The
interference effect spreads outward from the treated set and decays in graph
distance, and it is exactly zero at units with no treated neighbour. Both are
structural guarantees rather than learned behaviour, and we check them as unit
tests along with the additive identity.

\subsection{Scorecard}

Table~\ref{tab:scorecard} ranks the scenarios by displacement pressure
mitigation. With the burden term of \eqref{eq:dpi} active the contrasts are
legible. Market-led development raises $\DPI$ by $0.0172 \pm 0.0007$ relative to
baseline, while inclusionary housing and land-value capture lower it by
$0.0131 \pm 0.0006$ and $0.0125 \pm 0.0005$. The two mitigating policies are
close, and are ordered consistently across seeds with inclusionary housing
slightly ahead, which is unsurprising since it acts on rent and on stock while
land-value capture acts on rent alone.

Under the growth-rate form the same contrasts were of order $10^{-4}$ and
returned to zero within one step of the intervention. We stress that this is a
change in what the index measures and not in the simulated economy. The panels
are identical and only the reading differs.

\section{Discussion and open questions}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What we take from this}

Three points carry past the particular model.

Graph structure is not informative merely because it is present, even when the
generating process contains explicit spillover. The static intercept of
Section~\ref{subsec:collapse} shows that spillover which is permanent and early
is absorbed into the level of the receiving unit and becomes a fixed effect. A
benchmark built that way will report that graph models do not help, and the
report will be true of the benchmark and silent about the method. Anyone
evaluating a spatio-temporal architecture should check that the spillover
process is still moving inside the evaluation window.

The value of nonlinear message passing depends on the regime. In the untreated
equilibrium a linear spatial lag cannot be distinguished from \CGCEN{} at ten
seeds. Under intervention the gap opens to about $3.3$ MAE with large effect
sizes. Read practically, an agency running a steady-state monitoring model has
little reason to prefer a graph network to classical spatial econometrics
\cite{anselin1988,lesage2009}, whereas one comparing prospective interventions
with propagating consequences does.

Index design can settle a conclusion quietly. The growth-rate displacement index
is internally coherent and, applied to a permanent shift, reports approximately
nothing. An analyst who did not interrogate the functional form could reasonably
conclude that inclusionary housing has no displacement consequence, when the
index is simply unable to register a sustained level difference. The correction
in \eqref{eq:burden} changes the measured contrast by two orders of magnitude
without altering a single simulated quantity.

\subsection{Limitations}

\textbf{No full-scale empirical evaluation.} This is the principal limitation
and we state it without hedging: every quantitative result above comes from
synthetic data. We implement a complete empirical pipeline, which ingests a
census tract table carrying ACS and PLUTO fields, namely median gross rent
B25064, median household income B19013, population B01003, housing units B25001,
assessed land value, lot and residential area and a derived transit
accessibility score, builds tract adjacency from centroid geodesics by
$k$-nearest neighbours under a haversine metric, and emits a graph. We validate
it end to end. But we validate it on a schema-compatible sample and not on a
real extract. The loader reports its provenance through an explicit flag so that
the distinction cannot be lost downstream, and no number derived from that
sample appears anywhere in this paper.

The results therefore establish internal validity. Given a process with known
dynamic spillover, the architecture recovers it, and we can say when it fails
to. They establish nothing about external validity. Real tract panels are
irregular, sparsely observed, subject to boundary revision between censuses, and
carry confounding the generator does not simulate. Whether the conditional
advantage documented here survives contact with such data is untested, and we
would regard a policy application of \CGCEN{} before that test as premature.

\textbf{Scale.} Sixteen units over eighteen steps yields nine training windows.
That is small, and it interacts with the results in a particular way: capacity
is cheap to waste, which is part of why the temporal MLP is so variable, at
$\pm 7.97$ MAE, and why the linear model is competitive at all. Real
metropolitan graphs are larger, irregular, and have heavy-tailed degree
distributions which a regular lattice does not exercise. We do not claim the
ordering transfers unchanged.

\textbf{Interventions.} Policies are applied as multiplicative adjustments on
fixed sets with fixed timing. Real interventions are heterogeneous in intensity,
phased, politically endogenous to the neighbourhoods which receive them, and
often anticipated by markets before enactment. Endogeneity in particular would
break the exogeneity our design assumes, and addressing it needs identification
machinery, whether instruments, differences in differences with spatial lags, or
an explicit assignment model, which we have not built.

\textbf{The exposure mapping.} We assume interference travels only through the
one-hop summary of \eqref{eq:exposure}. This excludes longer-range effects not
mediated by intervening units, and it compresses a neighbourhood assignment
vector into a scalar. Both are standard simplifications
\cite{hudgens2008,forastiere2021} and both are falsifiable.

\subsection{Open questions}

The immediate priority is empirical replication. Obtaining a genuine tract
extract and rerunning the benchmark against it is the single most valuable next
step, and the loader exists so that this is a change of configuration rather
than a piece of engineering. A useful intermediate would be semi-synthetic: real
tract geometry, adjacency and initial conditions, with simulated interventions
layered on, which keeps the ground truth while introducing realistic structure.

Beyond that, three questions follow naturally. Richer exposure mappings would
relax the one-hop scalar towards multi-hop or distance-weighted summaries, and
it is not obvious how much this buys on a real adjacency. Uncertainty
quantification over the estimated effects would let the scorecard report
intervals on policy contrasts rather than point estimates; conformal and
ensemble approaches are the obvious candidates. Modelling the assignment itself
would carry the framework from the ex-ante setting, in which assignment is
stipulated, towards observational evaluation, in which it has to be estimated.

\subsection{Conclusion}

We have presented CivicTwin, a framework which splits urban policy effects into
a direct term and an interfering term under partial interference, with the split
enforced by the architecture rather than by regularisation. On a controlled
benchmark over ten seeds and four scenarios it outperforms a spatial lag model,
a graph-blind temporal MLP and persistence, and the topology ablations show that
the graph is carrying the advantage, since permuting the adjacency is worse than
removing it.

The advantage is conditional and we have tried to be exact about the condition.
\CGCEN{} separates from classical spatial econometrics under intervention, at
$p$ between $0.0015$ and $0.0033$, and not in the untreated equilibrium, at
$p = 0.127$. Under an earlier generator, in which the spillover arrived once and
stayed, it separated from nothing at all, and neither did anything else, because
spillover of that kind collapses into a static intercept. We report the negative
result beside the positive one because for anyone building spatio-temporal
policy models the boundary between the two regimes is more actionable than
either result taken alone.
